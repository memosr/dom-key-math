Every key you spend in the Den has the same expected return, whether you burn one per run or a hundred. Splitting them changes only two things: how long you sit at the keyboard, and how bumpy the ride gets.

Everything below comes from the formulas printed in the DoM whitepaper. Nothing is estimated.

Where each dollar goes

A key costs $1 and is cut four ways the moment you spend it. Three of those cuts can come back to you.

Weekly Prize Pool · 70c
70.0c
Your treasure scales linearly with keys, and so does your share of the pool. The pool size cancels out of the arithmetic entirely: what survives is your treasure rate against the field.
back per key = 0.70 x your treasure ratio

21.1c

The Throne · 5c
1.6c
The one place key count vanishes from the maths: more keys raise the drop chance in exact proportion, so the two cancel. Pool size is what is left, and it enters twice.
back per key = clear rate x floor(pool / 250) x 0.00005 x 0.80 x pool
Development · 7c
0.0c
The house cut. DoM 1.0 returned 90c of every key to players; 2.0 returns 93c.

The Throne is the only real lever

Pool size enters the Throne formula twice, once through the drop chance and once through the payout, so the return curves upward instead of climbing steadily. Below $250 it does not roll at all, and it steps rather than slopes because the formula floors the pool to the nearest $250.

Careful with this one. Those are snapshot numbers, not a promise. The Throne is funded by 5c of every key, so across its whole life it cannot pay out more than that. A pool only reaches $2,500 if nobody has claimed it for a long time, and the moment it gets there it becomes a race. High readings on this chart describe rare moments, not the average week.

A 1x cut is the single likeliest outcome at 45%, and four cuts in five land at 2x or below. Almost a third of a gem's average value sits in the 13x and 21x tails, which together show up once in every 67 gems.

Does the model hold together

A check worth doing on any economy model: add the parts back up and see whether they match what the designers said they were funding.

Blessings overshoot their funding. A player who reaches Kilnbane every single run pulls 21.1c per key out of a pool fed 18c per key. That gap is paid for by every run that ends before floor 10. Set the clear-rate dial to 85% and the two numbers meet.

The Throne undershoots, then overshoots. At the current $407 it returns 1.6c per key against 5c of funding. It reaches break-even at exactly $625, and everything above that is borrowed from the weeks nobody claimed it.

Add the designed shares and you get 93c. The live model lands near that only by coincidence at small Throne pools, where the Blessing surplus and the Throne shortfall happen to cancel. Move either dial and they stop cancelling.

What this model cannot tell you

Four gaps, left as gaps rather than filled with guesses.

  • Whether the Weekly pool is seededThe whitepaper does not say. DoM 1.0 launched with $5,000 placed into the Blessing Pool, so seeding is plausible, and it would change any attempt to read key sales out of the pool size.
  • What happens when the Blessing Pool runs thinDoM 1.0 described a self-balancing mechanism. The 2.0 whitepaper drops that language and does not replace it, so the behaviour of a 21x cut against a shallow pool is undocumented.
  • The real clear rateNobody has published what share of key spend actually reaches floor 10. It is the single input this model most depends on, and the dial above is a guess until the team shares it.
  • Buba CapsThe Arcade header lists them alongside Treasure, Blessings and Raffle Tickets. The whitepaper's rewards page does not mention them at all.
